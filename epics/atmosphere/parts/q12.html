The link on her phone: yes, it can work

  • The email carries both: a link, and the six-digit code under it.
  • Ana asks on her laptop; the laptop's page says "check your email" and waits.
  • She taps the link on her phone. The phone is signed in, and the laptop's waiting page signs in too, a moment later.
  • To stop someone tricking her into approving THEIR sign-in, the phone's page first says where the request came from ("Sign in on Chrome, on a Mac, in Boston?") with one button.
  • The sign-in rules don't get in the way: Spell runs the sign-in server, so the link signs her in there, and each device then starts its own fresh sign-in.

Email: what I can do alone, and what needs you

  • Alone: the mail code, its templates, and a local stand-in that catches every mail on your machine, so sign-in works end to end in tests and on your Mac.
  • Sending real mail from a machine you run: sent straight from a home connection, mail is mostly blocked or lands in spam (home addresses are on block lists, and most internet providers block the mail port). So your machine sends through a mail service, a relay.
  • From you, three things:
    1. Which machine, and how I reach it (its address, and SSH).
    2. The domain mail comes from (spell.spaces?), and adding three records to its DNS: I'll write them out, you paste them in (they prove the mail is really ours).
    3. An account at a mail service, and its API key. Signing up needs your name and a card, so it's yours to do; a free tier covers invites and sign-ins.
Built for sign-in mail, so it arrives fast and out of spam; free for the first few thousand a month. Its cost: one more account. Cheapest at scale. Its cost: an AWS account, and a wait for Amazon to let it send to anyone. No service. Its cost: only works from a rented server with a clean address, and still lands in spam often.

P3 builds the link-on-any-device sign-in and the local mail catcher now; real mail goes out once you've done the three things above.
