I'm really worried that as we add new features to the spell language that we will introduce ambiguity that would be hard for a novice to work with. Examples:
- a "card" is a spell-ui component, but a "card" in solitaire is app-specific.
- loading features from a library api might change the meaining of existing spell.
- upgrading the spell language itself might make your code unusable because we added a new spell feature that breaks one of your `to` rules.
- making alternatives for the language might introduce more ambiguity that we're not aware of
- it's extremely hard to know what exactly to write in spell, I often get confused myself. How can we guide the user to "the right" code, with and without an agent in play? (`agents-in-spell` is working on agent integration).
- how can we "explain spell code" in the editor without an agent prsent.
- how can we make language guides that are good for agents and humans? What do they look like? What would a "Programming in Spell" O'Reilly book cover?
- "how do I..." help in spell with or without an agent.
- how can we use verify (using an agent or not) from the compiler that the spell english as written is performing the most likely effect when looking at a line of code or a method, by using the english as natural language english and examining the output?
- how can we test all of the above in many languages?

Goal is that a novice can use spell fearlessly, and is guided to write the correct thing, without being expensive in terms of agent cost -- and we get a subset of that even without an agent running.

The risk is real, but narrow today: two rules read the same words, Spell keeps one, and says nothing.

  • Across the 33 examples, two rules read the same words 11,845 times. 6,071 of those are settled only by which rule Spell registered first (I17).
  • Eleven lines already do something other than they say, with no warning. The worst: a three-line library turns every heart in Solitaire black (I15).
  • The other ten: I4, I2, I11, I16, I5, I6, I10, I1, I7, I3.
  • It grows fast: 154 new ways to read a line in four weeks, and Spanish adds ties English doesn't have (I18).

More: the analysis's What's already going wrong and Where ambiguity comes from.

  • Spell never silently changes what a line means. A library, phrase or upgrade that would, says so first.
  • Your own words beat Spell's built-ins, and Spell says so once, where you declare them.
  • Spell says what it understood, from its own reading of the line, never by guessing at the English.
  • Nearly everything works with no agent. An agent is an optional, cheap layer on top that reads and never edits.

More: the analysis's The recommended direction.

  • The parser keeps every reading, not only the winner, and warns when two would compile differently. Most ties compile alike, so most stay quiet.
  • Adding a library or a view, or upgrading Spell, lists the lines whose meaning would change before it lands: a reading diff.
  • One rule for what a bare <card> names, shared by the compiler, the hover and the lint fix.
  • Every fix is Spell that already exists, such as its suit is hearts: no new Spell.

More: One word, two meanings, When Spell itself changes, and the analysis's Catching double meanings.

  • Plain words for every line, in the hover and Explain This. They're worked out from what Spell understood, so they can't just repeat what the English promises.
  • A red line offers "Did you mean", each choice saying what it does. Define moves last (I12).
  • One tested phrasebook: you want, you write, it means, and the near misses. It feeds the guides, the editor's help, spell explain and the agents' guide.

More: Explaining Spell without an agent, Guiding people to the right Spell, Guides for people and agents; and the analysis's Explaining a line, Guiding and Guides and the book.

  • First, warnings from the types Spell already knows: a yellow squiggle, one plain sentence, and the line to write instead. E.g. "A card is never jack, queen or king: those are ranks."

  • template: what a line like a "card" is a view makes
  • action: a line that starts with to
  • alias: another name you give something
  • built-in phrase: words Spell already knows
  • formula: a property worked out each time it's read
