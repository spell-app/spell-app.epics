Guiding people to the right Spell

Knowing what to write is the hardest part of Spell, for you too. This page shows real moments where the right line isn't obvious, what the editor offers today, and the shapes help could take, first with no agent at all.

Three times today's Spell leaves someone unsure what to write, and one of the ways silently does something else.

1. Joining words: five ways, three results

In a card's action, for the queen of spades:

You writeIt showsIt compiles to
print its rank && "of" && its suitqueen of spadescore.joined(..., " "), twice
print its rank & "of" & its suitqueenofspadescore.joined(...), twice
print its rank + "of" + its suitqueenofspades`${this.rank}of${this.suit}`
print its rank and its suitspadesthis.rank && this.suit
print "queen" and "spades"a list of two["queen", "spades"]
  • The examples don't agree. Tic-Tac-Toe writes its turn + " to play", its space inside the quotes; Checkers writes turn && "to play".
  • Copy Tic-Tac-Toe's shape, drop that space, and the board says "Xto play". Nothing warns.
  • The language's own page never says which to use: it shows && once, in an example, and never & or +.
  • and means three things: a list, between two written words; "both are true", between anything else; and in a sentence, just "and".
  • Even one file says one thing two ways: Solitaire's card says when clicked, its stock pile on click.

2. A line that reads as a question, but sets something

  • In a card's action, on a line of its own: the card is the queen of spades.
  • Spell reads it as "set": it makes a new name, card, and puts the word spades' "queen" in it, which is nothing.
  • No error, no warning. The same words after if ask the question, as the card's own test does: it is the queen of spades should be yes.
  • Beside it, its rank is queen on a line of its own is an error, "Don't understand".
  • Spell has a warning for this mix-up, "This sets ..., it doesn't ask", but only inside a yes-or-no answer's lines (it is droppable when), never in an action.

// to check a card

A phrasebook needs lines that are known to work. Spell already has thousands, all tested:

  • the parser's rule tests: about 965 English lines, each with the JavaScript it becomes
  • the syntax table made from them: 395 examples, each compiled to JavaScript, TypeScript and Python
  • 50 built-in steps and values (shuffle (a list), the length of ...), each with plain docs and an example
  • 33 examples, written to be copied; 15 have tests, 111 should be lines that run
  • 20 guides, each a walk through one example
  • the READMEs' examples, each run by yarn readme:test
  • Epic agents-in-spell already recommends the index this would read: Teaching agents to write clean Spell proposes ONE generated feature index, "each phrase, ability and element, with its one-line meaning, the words a person would search by, and the examples that use it". spell explain and the agents' look-ups (find_feature, find_examples) would read it.
  • The phrasebook is that same index, shown to people. This epic would ask for it to be built once, for both.
  • What every source lacks: the "you want" line. A test says what a line compiles to, not what someone was trying to do.
  • Inform 7, a language of English sentences that grows phrases, as Spell does:
    • two books: "Writing with Inform", by feature, and "The Recipe Book", by what you want to make
    • its Index has a Phrasebook: every phrase the story can use right now, an extension's too, each linked to its docs
  • Excel's Insert Function: "Search for a function", "Type a brief description of what you want to do". A "how do I" search over a fixed list, with no model. As you type a formula, its shape and each part's name show under it.
  • Python: "There should be one -- and preferably only one -- obvious way to do it" (PEP 20). Since 3.10, a misspelt name says Did you mean: 'print'?
  • Elm: a name it can't find lists the ones it knows under "These names seem close though:".
  • TypeScript's language server: "Did you mean 'length'?", with a quick fix, "Change spelling to 'length'".
  • AppleScript is often called easy to read and hard to write: your worry. Its dictionaries list each app's words, not how to put them together.

Five shapes. The first four need no agent; the fifth uses one only when they run out.

C. A "How do I ...?" phrasebook

Help › How do I ...? · proposed · 14 lines 
How do I ...?   [ join words                         ]

Join words into one text, with spaces between
  You write    its rank && "of" && its suit
  It shows     queen of spades
  Checked by   Solitaire's card test:  its name should be "queen of spades"

Join words with nothing between
  You write    its rank & its symbol
  It shows     queen♠

Watch out
  "and" between two written words makes a list:   "queen" and "spades"
  "+" adds no spaces:  write the space inside the quotes
  • Searched by the words people use: "join", "together", "space" find the same entry.
  • Every entry is tested: its line compiles, and a test says what it shows.
  • Opened from Help, and from a red line ("Show me how").
  • The same index the agents' look-ups read (above).
  • No agent. Size: large, most of it writing the "you want" lines. An agent could draft them; you'd review them.

  • Pick one way for each common job (joining words, a click handler, making a new thing), and nudge the others with a warning and a fix.
  • The way spell lint --fix moves a note from a line's end today.
  • Such warnings have two homes today (coherence review): the parser's warnings, which show in the editor and speak the file's language, and two lint-only checks in English. These would go in the first.
  • Each pick is a call about the language: yours, one at a time, its own double meanings shown first.
  • The examples would follow it. No agent. Size: small a job, medium for the first ten.

E. With an agent: "Ask Spell"

When the phrasebook has nothing · proposed · 9 lines 
How do I ...?   [ turn a card over when it's clicked   ]

No match in Spell's phrasebook.      [Ask Spell's agent]

a card
	when clicked
		turn it over

Checked:  it reads, and Spell knows "turn it over" for a card.      [Put it in]
  • Only after the phrasebook finds nothing, or when asked.
  • The agent gets the closest entries and the program's names, so it copies Spell's own shapes.
  • Spell checks its answer before it's shown: it parses, it compiles, nothing reads as a new name by accident.
  • Cost: about 0.1 cent an answer on Haiku 5.5, about 2 cents on Sonnet 5.5. My arithmetic, not measured: one call, about 8,000 tokens in (most of it the same instructions every time, so cached), 300 out, at the prices on Frontier models and what they cost.
  • What this epic would ask of agents-in-spell:
    • a "how do I" job among its flows (none there today), on the fast tier, with no edit tools
    • its answers checked as Change This's edits are, stepping up a tier when the check fails, as its question on sharing a job between cheap and strong models recommends (Q33)
    • the feature index, shared with people
  • Without an agent: A, B, C and D still work.
  • "The right Spell" has to be decided before any tool can point at it. The examples disagree today, and each pick is one of your calls about the language.
  • A did-you-mean that guesses wrong is worse than "Don't understand". For it is face card, the nearest phrase by words might be it is face up: it compiles, and asks something else. So each choice must say what it does, and never apply itself.
  • A silent line isn't an error, so a did-you-mean never sees it. the card is the queen of spades compiles; catching it is the job of Does the line do what it says?
  • A phrasebook is only as good as its "you want" lines: hundreds to write, and each language's own (Testing it in many languages).
  • Suggestions that list everything help no one: 185 at a blank line today. Fewer, whole, and right is harder than more.

Of the shapes above, which does this epic build first? The others can follow, in its plan.

Build the tested phrase index once; its first use is a did-you-mean on red lines, replacing "Define" as the only fix.

  • The phrasebook, the whole-sentence suggestions and the agent all read the same index later.
  • Fixes the worst thing today first: a typo's only fix makes it a new action.
  • Shared with agents-in-spell's feature index, so it's built once.
  • Cost: you'd see little until the index exists; the "you want" lines come later.

Help › How do I ...? first, as the answer to "I don't know what to write".

Cost: the biggest piece, mostly writing; near misses keep their "Define" fix meanwhile.

Settle which spelling Spell prefers for the common jobs, then build tools that point at it.

Cost: a run of language calls before anything ships; no tool helps meanwhile.

Let agents-in-spell's agent answer "how do I" now, and build the no-agent help later.

Cost: nothing without an agent, a cost per question, and no tested shapes for the agent to copy.

Where in the editor does someone ask "how do I ...?", whether a person or an agent answers?

Help › How do I ...? opens a panel that searches the phrasebook; a red line's "Show me how" opens it already searching.

  • One place to look, reached from where people get stuck.
  • "Ask Spell's agent" sits at the bottom of the panel, for when nothing matches.
  • Cost: one more panel in the editor.

Type "how do I join" on a blank line; the suggestions turn into phrasebook entries.

Cost: mixes a question into the code; hard to find unless you know it's there.

The console's line takes a question as well as a line of Spell.

Cost: the console runs lines on your app; a question there is a second meaning for that box.

The Spell Syntax guide grows a search box; the editor links to it.

Cost: leaves the editor, and the guide doesn't know your program's names.

When Spell has several ways to say one thing (&&, &, +; when clicked, on click), what happens to the ones it doesn't prefer?

Every way still works; the others get a warning saying what they do and a one-click change to the preferred way.

  • Only where the other way can surprise someone (+ on words), not for plain synonyms.
  • Each preferred way is your call, asked one at a time, its double meanings shown first.
  • Cost: a warning per job to write, and the examples rewritten to match.

The other ways become errors, with a fix, as retired words are today.

Cost: breaks programs people have written, and Python's "one obvious way" never went this far.

Every way stays quiet; the phrasebook and the guides show the preferred one.

Cost: nothing stops "Xto play", or and making a list.

For "how do I ...?", when does Spell call an agent, and what does that cost?

No match, or "Ask Spell's agent": the fast tier answers, Spell checks the answer before you see it. About 0.1-2 cents a question.

  • Most questions cost nothing: the phrasebook answers them.
  • The plumbing (which model, stepping up a tier, the check) is agents-in-spell's; this epic asks for the "how do I" job and the shared index.
  • Cost estimates are mine, from its models page's prices, not measured.

The agent answers every "how do I", reading the closest entries first.

Cost: a model call per question, and nothing without an agent.

This epic builds the no-agent help only; agents-in-spell decides the rest on its own.

Cost: the two may build two indexes, or an agent that ignores the tested shapes.
