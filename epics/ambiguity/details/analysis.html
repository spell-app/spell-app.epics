Ambiguity: the analysis

The seven review pages, pulled into one picture: how real the risk is today, where it comes from, what Spell already has, and the direction the pages point to, with costs and a first sketch for the plan.

agent ambiguity-analysis, for the session of epic ambiguity, after the seven review pages 2026-11-20
ambiguity -- a novice writes Spell fearlessly as it grows, mostly without paying for an agent. Seven review pages came back full of questions. You asked for an analysis instead: this page. Each of the seven keeps its depth, its questions now written as recommendations. A picture, nothing to answer. Its direction is the pages' recommendations, and your answers on One word, two meanings. No plan exists yet: section 7 is a sketch for one.
  • The risk is real, but narrow today. Across the 33 examples, two rules read the same words 11,845 times.
    • 6,071 of those are settled only by which rule Spell registered first.
      E.g. Calculator's clear it runs Calculator's own action, not Spell's clear, only because of that order.
    • 5,669 of those are harmless: two rules reading a property's name for the same job.
      E.g. a name like suit, which both rules turn into the same code.
    • A handful do different things depending on the winner, and nothing reports any of them.
      E.g. play the card runs Solitaire's own action, not Spell's sound, only because the sound's author set its level by hand.
  • Eleven lines already do something other than they say, with no error and no warning. Two are in Spell's own projects. The worst: a three-line library turns every heart in Solitaire black.
    • E.g. Solitaire's red if it is a diamond or it is a heart. Add a library that has a heart, and the line asks whether the card is that library's heart. It never is, so every heart card is black.
    • E.g. the card is the two of spades, in Solitaire's own card. It's always false: it compares the card with the "two" of the word spades, which is nothing.
  • It grows fast. Spell gained 154 new ways to read a line in four weeks, and Spanish adds ties English doesn't have. The parser keeps one reading and throws the rest away, so nobody sees a new tie arrive.
    • E.g. in Spanish, para cada artículo en sus artículos can start a loop, "for each item", or an action, "to each item". The loop wins, by order, in 21 lines of the examples, and nothing says so.
  • Nearly all of the fix needs no agent. The parser already knows every reading, every type and which rule read each line. Warnings, plain words and tested help can all come from that, offline, in both languages.
    • E.g. print the card is jack, queen or king. Spell already knows this compares a card with three words, so it could warn, with no agent: "A card is never jack, queen or king: those are ranks."
  • An agent is an optional top layer, and cheap. One real run on the fast tier caught every misreading it was shown, at about a fiftieth of a cent a line. It belongs on epic agents-in-spell's one agent client, not a new one.
    • E.g. shown print the card is the two of spades and its code, it said: "compares the card to a property two on the string spades, not checking rank or suit". Seven lines cost 0.12¢.
  • Start cheap and protective: fix the silent bugs, collect every reading, warn on ties and takeovers, then plain words in the hover and a tested phrase index (section 7).
    • E.g. the first fix: Solitaire's example becomes the card is the 2 of spades, which really checks the card's rank and suit.

Every case above has the same root: two rules read the same words, Spell keeps one, and says nothing.

The words get a second reading from seven places:

  • Spell UI has 157 bare names a drawing can use: card, item, list, table, step, message ... At least 9 of the 33 examples declare one of their own.
  • A bare <card> is your view if you declared one, else Spell UI's: turning a thing into a view later changes every drawing.
  • The compiler, the hover and the lint fix each decide this their own way.

More: One word, two meanings.

  • An import brings every name by default: its templates, values and quoted phrases.
  • Only a template declared twice is caught. A value word like heart, or a quoted phrase, is never checked.
  • A template always beats a value on purpose, so it is a heart flips to a template check the day any library has a heart.

More: One word, two meanings.

  • Each rule's author picks its level by hand. A to line with a blank loses to about 120 of Spell's rule files; only 22 step aside for yours.
  • So a new built-in with the same words as one of your actions takes them, silently: your upgrade worry is already true.
  • The language epic's upgrade phase covers wordings that LEAVE Spell, not new readings that ARRIVE.

More: When Spell itself changes.

  • 773 ways to read a line on 496 rules, against 619 on 407 four weeks before. 95 rules have more than one way; the wording rule alone has 62.
  • The outline and the sentences say every fact twice, so twice the places a new rule can collide.
  • Five ways to join words give three results, and the examples disagree on which to use.

More: When Spell itself changes and Guiding people to the right Spell.

  • is declares, asks or sets, depending on where the line stands: the root of the two silent "set or ask" bugs.
  • and makes a list between two written words, means "both are true" anywhere else, and is just "and" in a sentence.

  • Comparing two languages finds where they disagree, not where both are wrong: Solitario copies Solitaire's bad example faithfully, and passes.

One idea runs through all six areas: Spell says what it understood, from its own reading of the line, never from guessing at the English.

  • Each area works with no agent.
  • An agent, where it helps, sits on top, reads what Spell made of the line, and never edits.

What we'd build:

  • The parser keeps the runner-up, and warns when two readings would compile differently. Most ties compile alike, so most stay quiet.
  • Adding a library, a template or a new Spell UI view lists the lines whose meaning would change, before it's added.
  • A person's own to line or quoted phrase always beats Spell's built-ins, as Python's new keywords never take an old name.
  • One rule for what a bare tag names, shared by the compiler, the hover and the lint fix; the hover says when Spell UI has one too.
  • Every fix is a rewrite to a form Spell already has: its suit is hearts, <ui-card>, renaming in the project file. No new Spell.
  • Before a release: every change to Spell's rules lists the lines in every example, both languages, now read by a different rule; spell upgrade lists them for a person's project.

A sketch for the plan, not a plan. Cheapest and most protective first; each step works with no agent.

  1. Fix what's silently wrong today. The lint fix (I1), Solitaire's and Solitario's example (I4), numbers with commas (I6), the Spanish name (I7), spell explain's gaps (I14), inner names in plain words (I13). Plus a test that compiles every Spell block in the READMEs and guides (T1).
  2. Settle Spell's words for itself (section 8), before the warnings below get their text.
  3. Collect every reading. The parser keeps the runner-up; the census runs as a check, per language, its numbers on a page. The measure for everything after it.
  4. Warnings for ties and takeovers. Your own words always win (I16, I17); a warning when two readings compile differently (I15, I18); the check when a library arrives; a reading diff before a rule ships.
  5. Warnings for misreadings. The five type checks (I5 and its kin), "this sets, it doesn't ask" in actions (I2, I11), and the misreadings kept as tests in both languages.
  6. Plain words in the hover. "Spell reads this as ..." for every line, in Explain This too, and handed to its agent summary.
  7. The tested phrase index. Its first 20 entries with near misses, and a did-you-mean on red lines in place of Define (I12).
  8. Then the larger pieces. Help › How do I ...?, the reading diff in spell upgrade once the language epic's phase lands, and the agent checks on agents-in-spell's client.
