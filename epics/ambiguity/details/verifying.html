Does the line do what it says?

Some lines of Spell compile, pass spell lint, and do something other than what their English says. This page shows real ones from today's Spell, what already catches them, and the shapes a check could take: with no agent, and with one.

You asked for a set of review pages to read before any plan. This one is your question about checking, with or without an agent, that a line read as plain English does what its compiled output does. Six lines a novice could write about Solitaire's card. Four of them compile to code that is always false, and spell lint passes all four.

The line What a person means What it compiles to Caught today?
the card is the two of spades the card is the 2 of spades card === "spades".two: "the property two of the text spades" Only by TypeScript's check, and only with the TypeScript target. Solitaire's own card gives this line as its alias's example, in the comment under it "is the (rank) of (suit)"; Solitario's card does too, in Spanish.
the card is jack, queen or king its rank is one of those core.includes(["jack", "queen", "king"], card): the card itself among three texts Nothing catches it. its rank is jack, queen or king works, and so does the card is queen, which reads the rank.

the card is a face card and red both are true refused Yes: spell lint fails it, "and red" asks nothing: say what is red, e.g. "and it is red".
  • What they share: each misreading is a real reading of SOME line. The compiler took the one rule that matched, and that rule means something else.
  • One the compiler holds itself: Solitaire's auto play, line 107, set candidates to cards in the discard pile where the card is on top, then and the last card of each column on the next line.
    • The JavaScript joins the three lists, which is right.
    • The TypeScript output marks the result yes-or-no (as boolean), because "and" also joins conditions; TypeScript's check flags it. The compiler has two readings of this "and", and only one of them wrote the code.
  • README-spell's "sharp edge" no longer happens. It says the card is the queen of spades compiles to card === "spades".queen against a (suit) blank. Since epic spanish's P22 a blank reads its values singular or plural, and the line compiles to card.isTheRankOfSuit("queen", "spades"). A typo or a number word still lands in the same place.

Several things already stand between a novice and a misreading. None of them reads the line as English.

Lines nothing reads

  • A line no rule reads is an error where it stands: Don't understand "is the queen in spades".
  • spell lint fails on it, by file, line and column.

Warnings about what a line means

  • Some rules already refuse or warn on a line that reads but says nothing sensible.
    • Their words are in the parser's messages, in English and Spanish.
A line like What Spell says
the card is a face card and red "and red" asks nothing: say what is red, e.g. "and it is red"
cards in the deck where its suit is hearts and spades In a "where", "and" joins conditions, and "spades" isn't one: to put it with what the "where" finds, put the list and its "where" in parentheses
a condition that can never be true twice over One thing can never be both of these at once, so this finds nothing: did you mean "or"?
the name of the character's friend, when both could have a friend Read as the character's friend; the name of the character has a friend too ...: one of the few "two readings" warnings today
a apple, el carta Did you mean "an apple"?, ¿Querías decir "la carta"?

The types the compiler already knows

  • The compiler knows the type of each side of a comparison. Its own reading of the line, the Spell tree, says so.
  • It knows a card is being compared with a text, which can never be equal, and says nothing.

TypeScript's check, on the TypeScript output

  • A compile to the TypeScript target runs TypeScript's own check over what it wrote (typecheck()). On the lines above it found two of the misreadings.
  • But a novice never sees it:
    • it runs only with the TypeScript target; the editor runs the JavaScript
    • it says the TypeScript file's line, in TypeScript's words, not the Spell line
    • its errors are shown and never counted: the exit code stays Spell's (typecheckTargets())
    • the examples have their own, mostly from the runtime's loose types: 73 in Checkers, 26 in Star Wars, 6 in Solitaire
    • the runtime's core.includes takes anything, so the card is jack, queen or king passes it
  • It costs about 2.6 seconds more on a small project (5.7 against 3.2, the whole command).

The hover

  • In VS Code, a hover over a line shows the JavaScript its statement compiles to (hover(), its full detail). A programmer who looks sees card === "red".
  • The editor app's plain hover leaves the JavaScript out, on purpose. So a novice sees nothing wrong.

Tests, in English

  • its color should be black is a check; a failing one says in English what was wrong, and VS Code shows it as an error on its line (epic spanish's P22).
  • It catches only what someone thought to test.

The map from the output back to the line

  • Each compile writes a standard source map: each statement's first output line, back to its Spell file, line and column (SourceMarks).
  • So a TypeScript error, or an agent's verdict, could land on the Spell line. Today nothing uses it for that.
  • One check already catches "a line that reads, runs and lints clean, but means something else": the translator's ShapeCheck.
    • It compiles one statement on its own, in its scope, and compares the result with another's, its names and texts taken out (shapeOf()).
    • Today it only compares a Spanish line with its English one (Testing it in many languages).
    • It's the no-agent comparison to build on: one reading of a line against another, or the same line under old Spell and new (When Spell itself changes).
    • The language server compiles one statement too, for its hover (compileQuietly()): a second copy of the same step, which a plan would make one.
  • TypeScript warns on a comparison that is legal but can never be true: the TS2367 above, word for word our the card is red.
  • Python 3.8 added a warning for x is "a": legal, and almost never meant.
  • Go's go vet reports code that compiles but is probably wrong, e.g. a Printf whose arguments don't fit its format.
  • Inform 7, the English-like language for interactive fiction, shows what it understood in its Index: the world, the kinds, the phrases. The writer compares that with what they meant. Its problem messages quote the sentence back.
  • Elm's errors quote the line, say what they expected there, and suggest the likely fix.
  • Machine translation checks itself by back-translation: translate the result back, and compare. Reading compiled code back into English is the same idea.

Six shapes. The first five need no agent.

  • The compiler warns when its own reading is unlikely, from the types it already has:
    • a comparison whose sides can never be equal: a card and a text
    • a member read of a value that has no such member: a text's "two"
    • a thing compared with values of one of its properties: a card with jack, queen or king
    • a word one letter off a value an alias reads: quen for queen
    • a number with a comma and three digits after it: 1,000
  • What you'd see: a yellow squiggle, and in its hover one plain sentence and the line to write.
  • Cost: a phase. One check per comparison and member read, in the rules that make them; every message in English and Spanish; tests for each.
  • No agent: all of it, offline, on every keystroke the editor already parses.

  • Run TypeScript's check on every compile, in the background, then put each of its errors through the source map onto its Spell line.
  • Reword the few kinds that mean a misreading ("no overlap", "no such property on a text") in Spell's words; hide the rest, which are the runtime's loose types.
  • What you'd see: the same squiggle as A, from a different source.
  • Cost: half a phase, and 2-3 seconds of checking per compile. It finds 3 of the 5 misreadings above; not jack, queen or king, not 1,000.
  • No agent.
  • An alias's description often quotes a line using it. Compile that line, and check it reads through the alias it sits under.
  • Solitaire's card would warn at once: its -- e.g. "the card is the two of spades" reads as something else. Solitario's card says the same in Spanish.
  • Why it matters: the description is what the editor's hover shows, and what a novice copies.
  • Cost: small. Only 3 such examples in all the built-in projects today, and 2 of them misread.
  • A proposal, not Spell: "a quoted line after e.g. in a description is compiled". Its own double meaning: a description quoting a line to say "not this" would warn too.
  • No agent.
  • Before it picks, the parser gathers every rule that matched: then the highest priority wins, then the longest match, then the one written first (Choice.getBestMatch()).
  • A tie goes to the earliest rule, silently. Warn on a tie instead, naming both readings.
  • Cost: small to build, large to tune: nobody has counted how often rules tie today, and some ties may be on purpose. A count per language is on Testing it in many languages; the words themselves on One word, two meanings.
  • No agent.
  • Write the compiled code back in plain words, then compare what it is ABOUT with what the line is about.
    • The line: "the card" is the subject, "the two of spades" what it's checked against.
    • The read-back: "the card equals the property two of the text spades". Its object is a text, not a card: that difference is the signal.
  • The read-back itself belongs to Explaining Spell without an agent. This shape is only the comparison.
  • Cost: large, after the read-back exists; weaker than an agent reading the same two texts.
  • No agent.
  • A cheap model gets each line, the declarations it reads, and the JavaScript written for it. It answers in one line: OK, or what the person meant and what the code does instead.
  • It was tried once, on 2026-11-20, on Haiku 5.5:
    • 7 lines in English: it found 3 of 3 misreadings (the two of spades, jack, queen or king, and quen)
    • one false alarm: the card is up, because the prompt left out that a "two sided" view has a direction. It needs every declaration the line reads, built-in ones too.
    • 6 lines in Spanish: 3 of 3, imprimir 1,5 among them; but it missed bad Spanish (Testing it in many languages)
  • When it runs:
    • on request: "Check what this does", on a line or an action
    • over the examples and guides in CI, before anyone copies a misreading
    • on save, only if the person turns it on: the changed lines alone
  • What it may say: a warning on the line, marked as the agent's, never a change to the code. Dismissed, it stays quiet for that line.
  • Cached by the line's words plus its compiled JavaScript: the same pair is never asked twice, and a compiler change that changes the JavaScript asks again by itself.
  • Batched: one call per save, or per action, sharing one copy of the declarations, which the provider caches at a tenth of the price or less.
  • No agent: shapes A to E are the part that works without it.
  • What already exists to build it on:
    • Explain This, on the code's right-click menu, already asks Claude Code to explain a piece of Spell in plain words, with every tool turned off (ClaudeExplainer). The check is its sibling: explain the line, then compare with the code.
    • The voice check is the shape to copy: a fresh reader with no context, an answer in a fixed shape, and the reader handed in, so its tests run with no model (VoiceCheck).
    • Six places start Claude Code as a program today: the voice check, prompt acknowledgements, the language packs' fix, the translator, Change This and Explain This. Epic agents-in-spell plans one agent client for all of them; the check should be its seventh user, not a seventh copy.

  • Every misreading is a legal line. The compiler can only say a reading is unlikely; it can't know what was meant. So these are warnings, never errors.
  • A false alarm costs a novice more than silence: they learn to ignore the squiggles. The agent's one false alarm came from what it wasn't told.
  • A line can be fine alone and wrong in its action. Checking whole actions costs more, and finds more.
  • TypeScript's check sees the runtime's loose types, so some misreadings pass it.
  • An agent's verdict can change from run to run, or model to model. The cache keeps one; a new model may say otherwise.
  • Every warning needs its words in each language, and each language has misreadings of its own.
