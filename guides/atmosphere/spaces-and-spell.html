Spell and AT Protocol Spaces

Communities will run themselves in Spell, in spaces they can reshape. AT Protocol will keep their data and publish what they choose, so Spell doesn't have to reinvent hosting.

Written for: anyone working on or reviewing the epic atmosphere Status: draft the plan as it stands; nothing is built Researched on: 2026-11-01, by reading the specs. Nothing here has been run (see Sources)
  • An org's offices and events each get a public space and a private space.
  • Each of those is one AT Protocol Space, and pages, sheets and posts are records inside it.
  • A space has two roles: Lead, who can do anything in it, and Member, who reads, posts, comments and signs up.
  • Everyone in the org is a Member of its public spaces. Private spaces hold only their Leads.
  • AT Protocol enforces who can read a space. Every write goes through a Spell server, which checks it and writes as the org.
  • This plan covers org spaces. Personal spaces on spell.spaces are separate.
  • Nobody can write while Spell's server is down, and it holds the right to write for every org on it.
  • What someone writes stays with the org, and other apps see the org as its author.
  • An org with no host of its own lands on Spell's, so Spell does end up holding some data.
  • Spaces is an alpha, so Spell leans into it without depending on it yet.

Social is Spell's likely killer app: Facebook for your communities, with no ads, where you control the UI and the feed.

  • Love someone's art posts but not their politics? Change your feed's algorithm.
  • Got an event coming up? Add its pages, spreadsheets, docs and sign-ups to the group's space.
  • The spirit is myspace's, which spell.spaces brings to mind: making things on the web is fun again.

What the plan protects:

  • Not reinventing hosting: AT Protocol has worked out how to host and federate, and Spell's spaces must not end up fundamentally different from theirs.
  • Not holding data by choice: storing data is a possible sideline business, not the point.
  • Helping Spaces grow: killer apps and trivial fun ones, built in Spell, are a win for both projects.

Spell edits, a Space keeps, and public records publish.

Layer Its job Example
Spell Making, editing, running, and enforcing the rules The sign-up sheet's program, and who may change it
A Space Keeping the data, for those allowed in The group's sign-ups
Public records Publishing to anyone: signed, and rebroadcast by the network A guide, or a public status like Statusphere's

Each office and each event has a public space and a private space.

  • Public means everyone in the org, and anyone can see every public page.
  • Private means only that space's Leads.
  • No page or project gets a space of its own, so people only have offices and events to hold in their heads.
Role in a space Can
Member See everything, post, comment, sign up, and change what's their own
Lead See and act on anything: make and change pages, delete anyone's posts and comments, moderate

Who holds which role comes from the org's own roles, all of them temporary:

Org role Lead of Member of
Admin, Council Every space, public and private
Standing team lead Their office's two spaces All other public spaces
Event lead, event team lead That event's two spaces All other public spaces
Everyone else All public spaces

Three more rules:

  • A page can narrow who may use it.
    • By default every Lead of a space can change its pages.
    • A page like "manage attendees" can limit that to event leads and the council.
    • The other Leads can still see it, as a Space's read access is all or nothing.
  • An app can be stricter. Posting is the base model for a Member, and a particular app may narrow it.
  • Terms end on time. When a term ends, Spell takes the person off that space's member list, and the record of who held the role stays.

RS is one org, @rs, on an identity of its own that survives people coming and going.

  • Its standing teams are Administrators, Council, and team leads for membership, events and finances.
  • The events team also approves event proposals.
  • Each quarterly event has its own organizers: event leads and event team leads.

Managing a sheet happens in the event's private space. Signing up happens in its public one.

  • Seeing the sheet takes no Spell: the rows are in the public space, so anyone on its list reads them.
  • Signing up does: it's a write, so it goes through Spell, like every other.
  • A row with something private in it, such as a phone number, is kept whole in the private space, and Spell writes a copy of the visible part to the public one. Invitation requests work this way, without the copy.
  • There are no team spaces: every team's pages sit in the event's two spaces, for simplicity.
  • A team's public pages are visible to anyone, like every public page.
  • An event team lead sees every page in the private space, but a narrowed page may not let them change it.

A new event is copied from a template, and the template and these stages are a Spell program RS can change.

Invitations aren't a permission: they're a rule that counts.

  • How many someone may request depends on their standing in the group, plus what they volunteered for.
  • Tickets run out, and two people may ask for the last one at once.
  • A request goes into the event's private space through Spell, which shows the person only their own.

The first version trusts the page, and the server guards only the ticket count.

  • A Spell program runs in the browser, so a page could be made to ask for more than its allowance.
  • Organizers see every request, which is enough for a group that assumes everyone is a good actor.
  • A rule that runs on the server comes later. Running people's code on a server is an epic of its own.

Chat is one more app in a space: a thread is a page, and each message is a record.

A similar org runs its events in Discord, with threads for each audience. Most of them map straight across:

Thread Where it sits
Public, and the event's public thread The event's public space
Organizers, and team leads The event's private space
Event leads only A small extra private space, as a thread can't be hidden from the event's other Leads

What makes it more than an ordinary page:

  • Being told: Discord's real strength is the alert on your phone. Forwarding to email is a start, but not the same.
  • Volume: one account writes for the whole org, so a busy org's chat could reach the host's write limit.
  • Speed: Spell tells open pages at once, which suits chat, and live rooms are there if that's not enough.

Messages between people can be the same app, as a thread between two.
  • Behaviour-crisis notes: they go in the org's own private space, for Admins and Council. Whoever runs the server and the host can still read them, as nothing is encrypted, so RS should know that, or keep those notes elsewhere for now.
  • The recovery key: whoever leaves holding it holds the group's identity, so more than one person should. How the protocol does that is unchecked.
  • Leaving: a post staying with the group suits a community, but someone who leaves still needs a way to remove their own.
  • Number of Spaces: eight to start, plus two for each event, so about eight more a year, with everyone in the org on each public one's list.

The protocol enforces who can read a space. Spell enforces who can write what in it.

In Spell On AT Protocol
@rs, an org A dedicated DID (a permanent id), which can change hands without any one person's account
A public or private space One Space under that DID
Lead and Member A member list on each Space, with a read flag. What each role can write is Spell's
A page, a sheet, a post, a comment, a sign-up A record in that Space: each one added is a write

What a save does:

Reading is the protocol's job. Each org Space uses its member-list policy, so the host refuses anyone not on the list, with no Spell involved.

  • Everyone in the org is on the list of every public Space, and only Leads are on a private one.
  • Spell keeps those lists right as people join, change roles and reach the end of a term.
  • A credential already issued runs out within about 10 minutes.
  • Someone with an account keeps reading while Spell is down.
  • Each Space syncs on its own, so a page can follow just one event.

The exception is someone with no account. A member list can only name accounts, so Spell reads for them, from its copy, and checks their role itself.

Why Spell writes for the org, rather than everyone writing to their own repo as the protocol allows:

Everyone writes to their own repo Spell writes for the org
Enforcing the roles Apps can only ignore a record afterwards Spell refuses it
A Lead deletes someone's post It can only be hidden: it's in their repo It's deleted
Ana edits Bo's row It needs a merge of Spell's own It's the org's row
The last ticket, wanted twice at once Two repos, so nothing can stop both One repo, and the second write is refused
Ana, with no account Someone must make and host her one She signs in to Spell, by email
A stranger's program Gets what you granted, unless each has its own identity Asks Spell, which checks that program's rights
Spell's server is down People can still write Nobody can write
Ana leaves the org She keeps what she wrote It stays with the org
Who wrote it, to other apps The protocol says so The org; the person is only Spell's word, in a field

It's still a real Space, with a single writer.

  • The authority's own account may always read and write, under every policy.
  • It stays addressable, and can move hosts.
  • It does set aside the protocol's "authority rests in the author's DID".
  • People with their own accounts can write from their own repos later, as a Space allows many writers.

People use owen@spell and @rs: they read like email addresses, and they're friendly.

  • Nobody types or reads the at:// form.
  • Spell keeps the permanent id behind each name, since handles change.

owen@spell receives messages inside the community, and can forward them to outside email.

  • Each person chooses: no forwarding, one email per message, or digests.
  • Mail from outside doesn't come in.
  • Forwarding uses the same mail sender as the email-link sign-in.
  • A message is read by its sender and recipient, through Spell, in a space with no Leads. Whoever runs the server and the host can still read it, as nothing is encrypted.

Spell's declaration stays the only thing anyone writes, and Lexicon, AT Protocol's schema language, is generated from it.

  • Spell already generates JSON Schema from a declaration this way, so Lexicon is one more output.
  • Importing other people's Lexicons as Spell types is what makes remixing work.
Spell Lexicon What to do about it
a recipe is a thing A record, named like spaces.spell.x.recipe A name belongs to whoever controls its domain, and spell.spaces is ours
Text, date, list, one of String, datetime, array, known values Mostly direct
A decimal number None: floats are banned Store it as a string or a scaled integer
A thing inside a thing, saved once, then referenced A nested object, or an at:// link Each record is capped at 1 MiB, and whole-then-reference has no direct equivalent
Rename a field, upgrade on read Not allowed once published: new fields must be optional, with no renames or type changes Field names freeze only on publishing; a breaking change needs a new name
  • A draft in Spell can change freely, and the spec suggests marking a name experimental until it's published.
  • What a translated (Spanish) program's field names should be, in a record, isn't worked out.

Reused: DIDs and handles, hosting, OAuth, account moves, sync and moderation.

Spell runs:

  • spell.spaces: the site.
  • A space server: signs people in, checks roles, writes for each org, keeps each Space's member list right, and keeps a copy for pages and for people with no account.
  • A host, on standard software: for any org without one, invite only at first. This is the start of the sideline.

What the server has to get right:

  • The code is small: a few routes that sign in, check and write. The place server already carries data, and a room's referee already answers for who's in it.
  • Holding the right to write: a break-in at Spell could write to every org on it, so each org keeps its identity's recovery key, and can leave.
  • Staying signed in: how long that right lasts is up to each host, says the OAuth spec. It's only Spell's to decide on a host it runs.
  • Being needed: anyone can run the same code, so no org is tied to Spell's own.
  • Limits: one account writes for everyone. Bluesky's hosts allow about 1,666 new records an hour per account, and whether that covers Spaces isn't known.

The feed runs in the browser, over records from the server's copy, which is what makes "your algorithm" literal.

  • That should work for communities of hundreds, though it's untested.
  • Millions would need a server.

Programs never hold anyone's grant, because they ask the server.

  • What each program may do still needs a design.
  • Each gets its own web origin, so one can't read another's page.
  1. Writing without Spell: nobody can write while the server is down, and it holds the keys.
  2. Owning what you wrote in a group: it stays with the org when you leave, and other apps see the org as its author.
  3. Some remixing: other apps read only the spaces an org lets them.
  4. One identity everywhere, for people with no account: an email sign-in exists on one server only, until they attach an atmosphere account.
  5. Plain files: the stored form is binary archives, so export to plain JSON is something Spell writes.
  6. Confidentiality and recall: hosts and Spell can read everything, and what's been copied can't be recalled.
  7. Offline: a Space can't work offline, so a data folder stays the local copy.
  8. Instant updates from outside: anyone writing from outside Spell gets "something changed, go fetch". Live rooms stay separate either way.
  9. Freedom in the schema: published names freeze, and there are no floats.

Spell aligns with Spaces now, and keeps a way out until it's ready.

  • Its own words: breaking changes are coming, data may be lost, no security review, not for production.
  • Its hosted alpha server is invite only, and is deleted when the alpha ends.
  • Upgrades may not work: "Do not expect that you'll even be able to cleanly upgrade between versions".
  • It's a proposal, "not the final specification", hoping to launch later this year, with a beta around the end of November and no promised date.
  • A host that dies probably takes its Space data with it, as nothing mirrors it the way public records are.

The way out: a Spell host stays for local and offline use, shaped like a Space, with the same addresses and roles. Plain export works from the first version.

Unknown until it's run:

  • How a server holds an org's right to write, and for how long, on a host Spell doesn't run.
  • How to upload a file into a Space, and how big it can be.
  • Which sign-ins today's host offers. No email link turned up, but that source is from 2025.
  • Real prices for a paid host, and any independent security audit: neither turned up.

The window is open while they're in alpha, and they ask for reports of what's confusing or breaks.

  • Report back with a working app:
    • one server writing for a whole group
    • a public and a private Space for each office and event
    • sign-up without a password
    • pushing changes to pages
  • Offer Lexicon generation: Spell becomes an easy way to write schemas.
  • Build what they asked for, later: the recipes case "deserves a blogpost & an example app of its own", in their words. The family cookbook can be that example.
  1. Data folders' addresses: a full address like owen@spell/recipes, and a server's minimum polling time.
  2. Statusphere in Spell: Bluesky's example app. Public records only, so it needs no Spaces, and it tests the first real atmosphere write, OAuth against a real host, and using a schema someone else published.
  3. Publishing a guide: one public record, through the same write, using Standard.site, which describes a page and links to it.
  4. Lexicon generation: a Spell declaration becomes a published schema.
  5. The group trial: a throwaway host, a small space server, and one Spell group app modelled on RS: Leads and Members, temporary roles, public and private spaces, narrowed pages, one event from proposal to quiet, sign-ups, invitations, and a feed you can edit.
  6. Sign-in: an email link, with one check replacing today's local token for requests and room sockets alike.
  7. Keeping data safe: backups, export, delete and limits.
  8. Messages and chat: inside the community, with forwarding to email, as messages or digests.

A sheet's own "who may add" setting.

  • Companies will want it, and it fits the same check.
  • So does limiting a sheet to an event's attendees.

Not part of this plan: personal spaces.

  • Anyone will be able to create their own on spell.spaces, which has nothing to do with an org's spaces, other than perhaps linking between them.
  • family cookbook is saved in the cloud belongs with them, so that line keeps refusing for now.
  • A data folder stays what runs on your own machine, offline.
  1. Should every member get an account, so the protocol enforces reading for everyone? The plan says no: it would make Spell or a partner host them all.
