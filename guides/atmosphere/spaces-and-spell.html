Spell and AT Protocol Spaces

Spell will be where people and communities make things together. AT Protocol will be where those things are kept and published, so Spell doesn't have to reinvent hosting.

Written for: anyone working on or reviewing the epic atmosphere Status: draft the plan as it stands; nothing is built Researched on: 2026-11-01, by reading the specs. Nothing here has been run (see Sources)
  • Each person and each org has one space, with an address like owen@spell or @rs.
  • Underneath, a space is one main AT Protocol Space, plus one more for each private area in it.
  • Projects, events and teams are records inside a Space, never a Space each.
  • Everything a person adds is a write, and every write goes through a Spell server, which checks who may do it.
  • Four levels, viewer, editor, author and admin, are granted to roles, with terms, in parts of a space.
  • Spell generates Lexicon, AT Protocol's schema language, from its own declarations.
  • Nobody can write while Spell's server is down, and it holds the right to write for everyone on it.
  • What someone writes in a group stays with the group, and other apps see the group as its author.
  • Anyone with no host of their own lands on Spell's, so Spell does end up holding data.
  • Spaces is an alpha, so Spell leans into it without depending on it yet.

Social is Spell's likely killer app: Facebook for your communities, with no ads, where you control the UI and the feed.

  • Love someone's art posts but not their politics? Change your feed's algorithm.
  • Got an event coming up? Add its pages, spreadsheets, docs and sign-ups to the group's space.
  • The spirit is myspace's, which spell.spaces brings to mind: a place to establish any identity you want, from when making things on the web was fun.

What the plan protects:

  • Not reinventing hosting: AT Protocol has worked out how to host and federate, and Spell's spaces must not end up fundamentally different from theirs.
  • Not holding data by choice: storing data is a possible sideline business, not the point.
  • Helping Spaces grow: killer apps and trivial fun ones, built in Spell, are a win for both projects.

Spell edits, a Space keeps, and public records publish.

Layer Its job Example
Spell Making, editing, running, and enforcing the rules The sign-up sheet's program, and who may change it
A Space Keeping the data, for those allowed in The group's sign-ups
Public records Publishing to anyone: signed, and rebroadcast by the network A guide, or a public status like Statusphere's

Why this suits AT Protocol:

  • Its ethos post wants hosting generic, and each app owning its own product: Spell is the product.
  • It lists "skepticism of centralized control of any aspect of the user's experience" among its roots, which is the feed idea.
  • Its authors call a space a "shared social context", with a 100k-person forum as the design case.
  • A space can hold records of any type, so sign-ups, sheets and docs can all sit in one.
  • It wants the same data remixed across apps, and Spell lets people who aren't developers write those apps.

A space belongs to a person or an org, and Spell's word means the same as AT Protocol's.

In Spell On AT Protocol
owen@spell, a person An account (a DID, its permanent id), with a handle like owen.spell.spaces
@rs, an org A dedicated DID, which can change hands without any one person's account
A space One main Space under that DID, read by everyone in it
A private area of a space A further Space under that DID, read only by its own people
An open area: a project, an event, a team Records in the main Space, the area in each record's name
A post, a comment, a page, a sign-up A record: each one added is a write

Why a Space per private area, and not per project:

  • A Space per project is too heavy, and isn't needed: people think in one space, with parts.
  • Read access is all or nothing per Space, so a private page does need its own, and the protocol then enforces it.
  • Their design says a Space is cheap to make, and Spell makes these out of sight.

A person's space works the same way.

  • Their public page lives in the main Space, open to anyone to read, in the myspace spirit.
  • A document shared with a partner, or a trip writeup shared with a circle, each sit in a small private area.

People use owen@spell: it reads like an email address, and it's friendly.

One record, as a person sees it and on the wire · 2 lines 
owen@spell/family-cookbook
at://{spaceDid}/space/{spaceType}/{skey}/{authorDid}/{collection}/{rkey}
  • Nobody types or reads the at:// form.
  • Spell keeps the permanent id behind each name, since handles change.
  • A thing keeps its Spell address when it moves between a space's areas, though its record moves between Spaces.
  • It looks like an email address, but mail sent to it goes nowhere, unless that gets built too.

A grant says "this role has this level in this area".

Idea What it is Example
Level What someone can do Editor
Role A named group of people, named by the community, each with a term Council, for 2 years
Area A part of the space, its grants passed down to what's inside The space, then an event, then a team

Each level adds to the one before:

Level Can
Viewer Read, and add comments on existing posts and pages, and change their own comments
Editor Also add posts in existing pages and apps, and change their own posts
Author Also make new pages and apps, change existing ones, and delete anyone's posts
Admin Anything, including deleting anyone's pages, apps, posts and comments, and managing people
  • A row in a sign-up sheet, or a form's answer, counts as a post.
  • When a term ends, the grant ends by itself, and the record of who held the role stays.

The protocol decides who can read. Spell decides who can write what.

  • The protocol's member list has only a read flag and a write flag, with no idea of a post or a comment.
  • So everyone in a Space reads all of it, and the levels above are Spell's to check.

What happens on a save:

Why one writer, rather than everyone writing to their own repo as the protocol allows:

Everyone writes to their own repo Spell writes for the space
Enforcing the levels Apps can only ignore a record afterwards Spell refuses it
An author deletes someone's post It can only be hidden: it's in their repo It's deleted
Ana edits Bo's row It needs a merge of Spell's own It's the space's row
The last ticket, wanted twice at once Two repos, so nothing can stop both One repo, and the second write is refused
Ana, with no account Someone must make and host her one She signs in to Spell, by email
A stranger's program Gets what you granted, unless each has its own identity Asks Spell, which checks that program's rights
Spell's server is down People can still write Nobody can write
Ana leaves the org She keeps what she wrote It stays with the org
Who wrote it, to other apps The protocol says so The org; the person is only Spell's word, in a field

Reading:

  • Pages read from the server's copy, which also serves people with no account.
  • Someone with an account, or another app the space allows, can read a Space directly through the protocol, and keep reading if Spell is down.
  • Private areas are safe from that, as they're separate Spaces.

It's still a real Space, with a single writer.

  • The authority's own account may always read and write, under every policy.
  • It stays addressable, and can move hosts.
  • The protocol's authors suggest a similar shape for recipes: a space shared with applications, which "manage the access logic".
  • It does set aside their "authority rests in the author's DID".
  • People with their own accounts can write from their own repos later, as a Space allows many writers: Spell would count only what their level allows, using a "managed space", which asks an outside app about each person.

What the server has to get right:

  • The code is small: a few routes that sign in, check and write. The place server already carries data, and a room's referee already answers for who's in it.
  • Holding the right to write: a break-in at Spell could write to every space on it, so each owner keeps their identity's recovery key, and can leave.
  • Staying signed in: how long that right lasts is up to each host, says the OAuth spec. It's only Spell's to decide on a host it runs.
  • Being needed: anyone can run the same code, so nobody is tied to Spell's own.
  • Limits: one account writes for everyone. Bluesky's hosts allow about 1,666 new records an hour per account, and whether that covers Spaces isn't known.
  • Big spaces: the draft's change feed shows no way to ask for one project's changes, so the server keeps a copy, and answers pages from it.

Sharing a thing with particular people makes a private area for it, out of sight.

  • Only Spell writes, so moving a thing into a private area is a small rewrite, and its address stays the same.
  • Changing who's in that area is a change to one list, with nothing moved.
  • Two audiences on one thing become one area holding both, so they see each other's comments, and the sharing screen must say so.
In owen@spell Where it sits Who can do what

A document with a partner A private area of two Both are authors
A trip writeup for a circle A private area, listing a friend list The circle are viewers

Friend lists, which the people library already has, can stand in for a list of names.

RS is one org with one space, @rs, on a dedicated identity that survives admins coming and going.

It's a real community, with quarterly events run by a rotating team, and its members all trust each other.

  • The roster is a page drawn from the role lists, so there's one list, not two.
  • Every organizer sees and changes everything in the organizers' area, as RS is a trusted group. An area per team would add nothing.
  • A member can write where they can't read: Spell puts their invitation request in the organizers' area, and shows them only their own.

The template and these stages are a Spell program RS can change, rather than a product's fixed settings.

Invitations aren't a permission: they're a rule that counts.

  • How many someone may request depends on being a friend or a member, plus what they volunteered for.
  • Tickets run out, and two people may ask for the last one at once.
  • A friend is a viewer, who can't add posts, so a request is its own kind of record, open to viewers.

The first version trusts the page, and the server guards only the ticket count.

  • The page works out someone's allowance, and could be made to ask for more, as a Spell program runs in the browser.
  • Organizers see every request, which is enough for a group that assumes everyone is a good actor.
  • A rule that runs on the server comes later, for groups that can't assume that. Running people's code on a server is an epic of its own.
  • Behaviour-crisis notes: a private area keeps them from members and organizers, but whoever runs the server and the host can still read them, as nothing is encrypted. RS should know that, or keep those notes elsewhere for now.
  • The recovery key: whoever leaves holding it holds the group's identity, so more than one person should. How the protocol does that is unchecked.
  • Leaving: a post staying with the group suits a community, but someone who leaves still needs a way to remove their own.

family cookbook is saved in the cloud means: in the signed-in person's own space.

  1. Ana writes the line, and adds a recipe. It stays in her browser, and the page says so.
  2. She signs in with an email link, and has ana@spell.
  3. Her recipe goes up, into her space.
  4. She opens it on her phone, signs in, and it's there.
  5. She hits a limit: the save is refused, says why, and nothing's lost.
  6. She leaves: she downloads her data as plain JSON, or moves her space to another host.

A data folder stays what runs on your own machine, offline, with the same addresses.

Spell's declaration stays the only thing anyone writes, and Lexicon is generated from it.

  • Spell already generates JSON Schema from a declaration this way, so Lexicon is one more output.
  • Importing other people's Lexicons as Spell types is what makes remixing work.
Spell Lexicon What to do about it
a recipe is a thing A record, named like spaces.spell.x.recipe A name belongs to whoever controls its domain, and spell.spaces is ours
Text, date, list, one of String, datetime, array, known values Mostly direct
A decimal number None: floats are banned Store it as a string or a scaled integer
A thing inside a thing, saved once, then referenced A nested object, or an at:// link Each record is capped at 1 MiB, and whole-then-reference has no direct equivalent
Rename a field, upgrade on read Not allowed once published: new fields must be optional, with no renames or type changes Field names freeze only on publishing; a breaking change needs a new name
  • A draft in Spell can change freely, and the spec suggests marking a name experimental until it's published.
  • What a translated (Spanish) program's field names should be, in a record, isn't worked out.

Reused: handles and DIDs, hosting, OAuth, account moves, member lists, sync and moderation.

Spell runs:

  • spell.spaces: the site.
  • A space server: it signs people in, checks levels, writes for each space, and keeps a copy to answer pages from.
  • A host, on standard software: for anyone who hasn't got one, invite only at first. This is the start of the sideline.

Who holds the data depends on who hosts the space.

  • An org's data sits on the org's host, and its members need no account.
  • A person's own space needs an account, which someone has to host: Spell's host, until they choose another.
  • The identity and data can move to another host, so nobody is stuck.

The feed runs in the browser, over records from the server's copy, which is what makes "your algorithm" literal.

  • That should work for communities of hundreds, though it's untested.
  • Millions would need a server.

Programs never hold anyone's grant, because they ask the server.

  • What each program may do still needs a design.
  • Each gets its own web origin, so one can't read another's page.
  1. Writing without Spell: nobody can write while the server is down, and it holds the keys.
  2. Owning what you wrote in a group: it stays with the org when you leave, and other apps see the org as its author.
  3. Some remixing: other apps read only what a space lets them, and private areas not at all.
  4. One identity everywhere, for people with no account: an email sign-in exists on one server only, until they attach an atmosphere account.
  5. Plain files: the stored form is binary archives, so export to plain JSON is something Spell writes.
  6. Confidentiality and recall: hosts and Spell can read everything, and what's been copied can't be recalled.
  7. Offline: a Space can't work offline, so a data folder stays the local copy.
  8. Instant updates from outside: anyone writing from outside Spell gets "something changed, go fetch". Live rooms stay separate either way.
  9. Freedom in the schema: published names freeze, and there are no floats.

Spell aligns with Spaces now, and keeps a way out until it's ready.

  • Its own words: breaking changes are coming, data may be lost, no security review, not for production.
  • Its hosted alpha server is invite only, and is deleted when the alpha ends.
  • Upgrades may not work: "Do not expect that you'll even be able to cleanly upgrade between versions".
  • It's a proposal, "not the final specification", hoping to launch later this year, with a beta around the end of November and no promised date.
  • A host that dies probably takes its Space data with it, as nothing mirrors it the way public records are.

The way out: a Spell host stays for local and offline use, shaped like a Space: same addressing, same member list. Plain export works from the first version.

Unknown until it's run:

  • How a server holds a space's right to write, and for how long, on a host Spell doesn't run.
  • How to upload a file into a Space, and how big it can be.
  • Which sign-ins today's host offers. No email link turned up, but that source is from 2025.
  • Real prices for a paid host, and any independent security audit: neither turned up.

The window is open while they're in alpha, and they ask for reports of what's confusing or breaks.

  • Build what they asked for: the recipes case "deserves a blogpost & an example app of its own", in their words. The family cookbook can be that example.
  • Report back with a working app:
    • sharing one thing, by small private Spaces
    • one server writing for a whole group
    • sign-up without a password
    • pushing changes to pages
  • Offer Lexicon generation: Spell becomes an easy way to write schemas.
  1. Data folders' addresses: a full address like owen@spell/recipes, one per person with personal "recipes", and a server's minimum polling time.
  2. Statusphere in Spell: Bluesky's example app. Public records only, so it needs no Spaces, and it tests the first real atmosphere write, OAuth against a real host, and using a schema someone else published.
  3. Publishing a guide: one public record, through the same write, using Standard.site, which describes a page and links to it.
  4. Lexicon generation: a Spell declaration becomes a published schema.
  5. The group trial: a throwaway host, a small space server, and one Spell group app modelled on RS: levels, roles with terms, private areas, one event with its teams, sign-ups, invitations, and a feed you can edit.
  6. Sign-in: an email link, one check replacing today's local token for requests and room sockets alike.
  7. Keeping data safe: backups, export, delete and limits. Then the cloud line compiles.
  8. The cookbook: the example the protocol's authors asked for, written up for them.
  1. Can friends sign up on sheets? The plan has them as viewers, so only invitation requests.
  2. Can members see team pages? The plan keeps them private to organizers, with event pages open.
  3. Is one level per role enough, or does a sheet need its own "who may add" setting?
  4. Does a person's space wait for them to choose a host, or does Spell host it from the start? The plan hosts it.
  5. Should owen@spell receive mail?
