Spell and AT Protocol Spaces

Spell is where people make and edit things, for themselves and their communities. AT Protocol is where those things are kept and published, so Spell doesn't have to reinvent hosting.

Written for: Owen, to comment on, before the epic's Overview is rewritten Status: draft a design under discussion; nothing is built Researched on: 2026-11-01, by reading the specs. Nothing here has been run (see Sources)

You've decided or agreed:

  • Social may be Spell's killer app, so lean into what Spaces promises, without committing to it yet.
  • Each person has a space, and each org has a space. Each is one AT Protocol Space.
  • A project is records inside a space, never a space of its own.
  • People see owen@spell, never an at:// address.

I recommend:

  • All reads and writes go through a Spell server, which enforces every rule.
  • Each thing in a space can have its own list of people.
  • Spell generates Lexicon, AT Protocol's schema language, from its own declarations.
  • Spell's server becomes required, and holds the right to write for everyone on it.
  • Privacy inside a space is Spell's word, not the protocol's.
  • Giving every person a space means someone hosts them all, and by default that's Spell: the thing you'd rather not do.

"Imagine Facebook, but for your communit(ies), without ads, and where you can control the UI and the feed algorithm. Want to see more or less of one person or group's stuff? Love their art posts but hate their politics? Just change your feed algorithm. Want to add pages (spreadhseets, docs, sign-ups, organizer-specific pages) for an event you have coming up? Just add them to the group's space."

"I don't want to have to re-think everytying they have already figured out in terms of how to host/federate/etc. Our 'spaces' being fundamentally different than theirs would be a huge loose."

  • The name: you've bought spell.spaces. It brings myspace to mind: the first place you could establish any identity you wanted, back when making things on the web was fun because it wasn't so complicated.
  • Storing data: you're not excited about holding people's data, but it could be a lucrative sideline you own.
  • The bigger win: killer apps, or trivial fun ones, in spell.spaces help Spaces grow.

Spell edits, a Space keeps, and public records publish.

Spell Making, editing, running, and enforcing the rules The sign-up sheet's program, and who may change it
A Space Keeping the data, for those allowed in The group's sign-ups

  • Why it suits AT Protocol: its ethos post wants hosting generic and each app owning its own product. Spell is the product, and hosting stays theirs.
  • Why it suits your feed: that post also lists "skepticism of centralized control of any aspect of the user's experience" among its roots.
  • Why it suits a community: Holmgren calls a space a "shared social context". A 100k-person forum is his design case, and a space can hold records of any type, so sign-ups, sheets and docs can all sit in one.
  • What Spell adds: they want the same data remixed across apps, but building one still takes a developer. Spell lets anyone write one.
  • Each person has a space, and each org has a space: that's the word people know, and it stays.
  • Underneath, each is one AT Protocol Space, so Spell's word and theirs mean the same thing.
Spell AT Protocol
owen@spell, a person An account (a DID, its permanent id), with a handle like owen.spell.spaces
@spell, an org A dedicated DID that owns spaces, and can change hands without any one person's account
Any space, a person's or an org's A Space, named by its authority, type and key
A project in a space Records in that Space, the project in each record's name
Viewer, editor, author, admin Nothing: the protocol has only a read flag and a write flag, so these are Spell's
  • Levels, as I read your order: viewer reads; editor changes what's there; author also makes new apps and pages; admin manages people.
    • They apply in the org, then again in each app.
    • They replace today's guest, member, admin.
  • Later, for people who bring their own account:
    • A "managed space" asks an outside app about each person, and Spell could be that app.
    • Custom policies are explicitly allowed too.
  • Why not a Space per project: it's too heavy for people to think about. Their design says a Space is cheap to make, so the reason is simplicity, not cost.

People use owen@spell: it reads like an email address, and it's friendly.

at://{spaceDid}/space/{spaceType}/{skey}/{authorDid}/{collection}/{rkey}
  • Nobody types or reads the at:// form.
  • Spell keeps the permanent id behind each name, since handles change.
  • The catch: it looks like an email address, but mail sent to it goes nowhere, unless we make it work.

Your idea: a space's records are all written by that space's own account, and Spell holds the right to do that, so it can enforce any rule.

Compared with how AT Protocol means Spaces to work:

Everyone writes to their own repo Everything goes through Spell
Ana edits Bo's row No: it needs a merge of our own Yes: it's the space's row
Ana joins an org with no account Someone must make and host her one Fine: she only signs in to Spell
A stranger's program Gets what you granted, unless each has its own identity Asks Spell, which checks that program's rights
Rules finer than the whole space Not possible Any rule
Pages hear about changes Needs a service to pass it on At once
The server is down People can still write Nobody can read or write through it
Ana leaves the org She keeps what she wrote It stays with the org
Who wrote it, to other apps The protocol says so The org; the person is only Spell's word, in a field

Is it still a real Space? Yes, as far as I can read, but one with a single writer.

  • The authority's own account is always allowed to read and write, under every policy.
  • It stays addressable, and can move hosts.
  • Holmgren suggests the same shape for recipes: put them in a space shared with applications, and "let those applications manage the access logic".
  • It gives up his "authority rests in the author's DID".
  • People with their own accounts can write from their own repos later, because a Space allows many writers.

"Pretty straightforward, no?" The code is; the promise isn't.

  • The code: a few routes that sign in, check a level and write. The place server already carries data, and a room's referee already answers for who's in it.
  • Holding the right to write: a break-in at Spell can write to every space on it. Each space's owner should keep its identity's recovery key, so they can leave.
  • Staying signed in: how long that right lasts is up to each host, says the OAuth spec. It's only ours to decide on a host we run.
  • Being needed: nothing works through it while it's down. It can be the same code anyone can run, so nobody is tied to us.
  • Limits: one account writes for everyone.
    • Bluesky's hosts allow about 1,666 new records an hour per account, by the figure in our own code.
    • I don't know if that covers Spaces, and our own host could set its own.
  • Big spaces: a page can't ask for one project's changes, as the draft's change feed shows no such filter. So the server keeps a copy and answers pages from it.

Privacy inside a space is needed, and it's the one case Spaces handles badly: read access is all or nothing, and Holmgren names a different audience for each recipe as the poor fit.

Two ways to do it:

A: Spell checks each thing's list B: A hidden Space per audience
Who enforces it Spell, on every read and write The protocol
Changing a thing's audience Change its list Move its records to another Space
Two audiences at once Both on the list A record is in one Space only

  • Each thing can have its own list of people; with none, it follows the space's.
  • A list can name a person, an org, or a friend list, which the people library already has.
  • The list's shape goes in now. Checking it comes after the group app, as you said of finer rules: "make sure it'll be easy to add but don't work on it now".
  • The price: your privacy only holds while reads go through Spell. Hosts can still read everything, either way.
  • Still to design: Holmgren warns that two audiences on one thing raises whether they see each other's comments. With one list, they do.
  • Not this: "love their art, hate their politics" isn't privacy at all. It's a filter on your side, and needs none of this.

The two unify by generating Lexicon, not by replacing either side. Spell's declaration stays the only thing anyone writes.

  • Spell already generates JSON Schema from a declaration this way, so Lexicon is one more output.
  • Importing other people's Lexicons as Spell types is what makes remixing work.
Spell Lexicon Problem
a recipe is a thing A record, named like spaces.spell.x.recipe A name's owner is whoever controls its domain, so you own this one now you've bought it
Text, date, list, one of String, datetime, array, known values Mostly direct
A decimal number None Floats are banned, so store them as strings or scaled integers
A thing inside a thing, saved once, then referenced A nested object, or an at:// link Each record is capped at 1 MiB, and whole-then-reference has no direct equivalent
Rename a field, upgrade on read Not allowed once published New fields must be optional, with no renames or type changes; breaking changes need a new name
  • Publishing is the line: a draft in Spell can change freely, and field names freeze only when published. The spec suggests marking a name experimental until then.
  • Records in a space only Spell writes: I expect these can change more freely than public ones, as nobody else depends on them yet. I haven't confirmed that.
  • Not worked out: what a translated (Spanish) program's field names should be, in a record.

Reuse rather than rethink: handles and DIDs, hosting, OAuth, account moves, sync and moderation.

Spell would run:

  • spell.spaces: the site.
  • A space server: it signs people in, checks levels and lists, writes for each space, and answers pages from its copy.
  • A host, on standard software: for anyone who hasn't got one.

Who holds the data? It depends on who hosts the space, and this is where the goal gets tested.

  • A member of an org needs no account, so the org's host holds the org's data.
  • A person's own space does need an account, which someone has to host.
  • So anyone without a host lands on Spell's: your sideline stops being optional, unless a person's space can wait until they choose one.
  • What makes it bearable: the identity and data can move to another host, so nobody is stuck with us.

The feed: running it in the browser, over records from the server's copy, is what makes "your algorithm" literal.

  • That should work for communities of hundreds.
  • I haven't tested how far it scales, and millions would need a server.

Programs: they never hold your grant, because they ask the server.

  • What each program may do still needs a design.
  • Each still wants its own web origin, so one can't read another's page.

Given the design above, with the costs of going through Spell listed first:

  1. Independence from Spell: nothing works through the server while it's down, and it holds the keys.
  2. Ownership of what you wrote in a group: it stays with the org when you leave, and other apps see the org as its author.
  3. Some of the remixing: another app can only read a space if Spell lets it, because a credential shows the whole space. This is the "atmospheric" part they care about most.
  4. One identity across servers, for people with no account: an email sign-in exists on one server only. Attaching an atmosphere account later would carry them across.
  5. Plain files: the stored form is binary archives, so export to plain JSON becomes something we write.
  6. Confidentiality and recall: hosts and Spell can read everything, and anything already copied can't be recalled.
  7. Offline: a data folder remains the local copy.
  8. Instant updates between servers: "something changed, go fetch" still applies for anyone writing from outside Spell. Rooms stay separate either way.
  9. Freedom in the schema: published names freeze, and there are no floats, as the schema section says.

You want to lean in without committing, and these are the reasons that's right.

  • Its own words: breaking changes are coming, data may be lost, no security review, not for production.
  • Its hosted alpha server is invite only, and is deleted when the alpha ends.
  • Upgrades may not work: "Do not expect that you'll even be able to cleanly upgrade between versions".
  • It's a proposal, "not the final specification". It hopes to launch later this year, and to reach a beta around the end of November, with no promised date.
  • A host that dies probably takes its Space data with it, as nothing mirrors it the way public records are.

Unknown until we run it:

  • How a server holds an org's right to write, and for how long, on a host we don't run.
  • How to upload a file into a Space, and how big it can be.
  • Which sign-ins today's host offers. I found no email link, but that source is from 2025.
  • Real prices for a paid host, and any independent security audit: I found neither.

The window is open while they're in alpha, and they ask for reports of what's confusing or breaks.

  • Build what they asked for: Holmgren says the recipes case "deserves a blogpost & an example app of its own". The family cookbook could be that example.
  • Report back with a working app:
    • sharing one thing, without a Space per audience
    • records people edit together
    • sign-up without a password
    • pushing changes to pages
  • Offer Lexicon generation: Spell becomes an easy way to write schemas.
  1. Statusphere in Spell: Bluesky's example app. Public records only, so it needs no Spaces, and it tests the first real atmosphere write, OAuth against a real host, and using a schema someone else published.
  2. Trial: a throwaway host, a small space server writing for one org, and one Spell group app with posts, comments, a sign-up sheet and a feed you can edit, built from a Lexicon that Spell generates.
  3. Seam: a Spell host stays for local and offline use, shaped like a Space: same addressing, same member list.
  4. Sharing: each thing's list, once the group app works.

Your call:

  1. Through Spell, reads and writes? I'd say yes, keeping own-repo writers possible later.
  2. Are the four levels right, as I read them in "Spaces, people and orgs"?
  3. The first app: a group space with an editable feed, or the cookbook? I'd build the group space.
  4. Sharing one thing: after the group app, or in the first version? I'd say after.
  5. A person with no host: does Spell host their space, or does it wait until they choose one? I'd host it, as the first version of your sideline, and invite only.
