Spell and AT Protocol Spaces

Communities will run themselves in Spell, in spaces they can reshape. AT Protocol will keep their data and publish what they choose, so Spell doesn't have to reinvent hosting.

Written for: anyone working on or reviewing the epic atmosphere Status: draft the plan as it stands; nothing is built Researched on: 2026-11-01, by reading the specs. Nothing here has been run (see Sources)

spell.spaces's overall goal is helping small to medium-sized groups get organized.

It provides a layered structure to manage who can see what within the org, separating public data, visible to members, from private data, visible to the organizers of any particular area.

  • Each organization gets a number of public and private spaces within it, and each is a distinct AT Protocol Space.
    • For example, each standing team and each event gets both a public space and a private space.
  • Everyone in an org has an AT Protocol account, and signs in with it.
  • Pages, sheets, posts and the Spell code behind them are all records inside a space.
  • A space starts with two roles, and each org can change them:
    • Members can read anything public in the org, make new posts and comments, and edit their own contributions.
    • Leads are the only people who can reach a private space. They can make new pages or install apps in either space, and delete, but not edit, anyone's posts and comments.
    • Leads also manage who's in their spaces. By default they can make any current Member a Lead, and remove other Leads.
  • The protocol enforces who can read a space. Spell's write server guards every write, and writes as the org.
  • Private data reaches other people by projection: Spell writes a view of it into a Space they can see.
  • This plan covers org spaces. Personal spaces on spell.spaces are separate.

Social is Spell's likely killer app: Facebook for your communities, with no ads, where you control the UI and the feed.

  • Love someone's art posts but not their politics? Change your feed's algorithm.
  • Got an event coming up? Add its pages, spreadsheets, docs and sign-ups to the group's space.
  • The spirit is myspace's, which spell.spaces brings to mind: making things on the web is fun again.

What the plan protects:

  • Not reinventing hosting: Spell's spaces stay real AT Protocol Spaces, so it reuses how they host and federate.
  • Not holding data by choice: storing data is a possible sideline business, not the point.
  • Helping Spaces grow: killer apps and trivial fun ones, built in Spell, are a win for both projects.

An org has a number of public and private spaces, each a distinct AT Protocol Space. Each standing team and each event gets both a public space and a private space.

  • Public means everyone in the org.
  • Private means only that space's Leads.
  • No page or project gets a space of its own, so there are only teams and events to hold in your head.
  • The worked example below draws this.
Role in a space Can
Member Read anything public in the org, make new posts and comments, sign up, and edit their own contributions
Lead Reach the private space. Make new pages and install apps in either space, change existing ones, delete but not edit anyone's posts and comments, manage who's in the space, make any current Member a Lead, and remove other Leads

Who holds which role comes from the org's own roles, all of them temporary:

Org role Lead of Member of
Admin, Council Every space, public and private
Standing team lead Their team's two spaces All other public spaces
Event lead, event team lead That event's two spaces All other public spaces
Everyone else All public spaces

The rules around them:

  • An org can change the roles: it can rename them and add more, and the protocol only cares who's on a Space's list.
  • A role can have its own rules: an org can say who's eligible for it, and who may grant it. Making someone an event lead may take the standing events team's lead, and Spell refuses anyone else, whoever a Lead names.
  • Admin and Council can't be removed by a space's Leads: those come from the org's own roles, so a falling-out inside one space can't lock everyone out.
  • An app can set its own rules: this is the base model, and a particular app may narrow it, as a sheet's manage page can change a row where a post couldn't be edited.
  • A page can narrow who may change it: "manage attendees" can be for event leads and the council. The other Leads can still see it, as a Space's read access is all or nothing.
  • Terms end on time: Spell takes the person off that space's member list, and the record of who held the role stays.
  • Leaving doesn't take anything away: someone leaving a role or an org can't write any more or see private spaces, and what they added stays.

The protocol enforces who can read a space. Spell enforces who can write what in it.

In Spell On AT Protocol
@rs, an org A dedicated DID (a permanent id), which can change hands without any one person's account
A person An account, on any host
A public or private space One Space under the org's DID
Lead and Member A member list on each Space, with a read flag. What each role can write is Spell's
A page, a sheet, a post, a comment, a sign-up A record in that Space: each one added is a write

Reading is the protocol's job.

  • Each org Space uses the member-list policy, so the host refuses anyone not on the list.
  • A public Space's list holds everyone in the org, and a private Space's holds its Leads.
  • Spell keeps those lists right as people join, change roles and reach the end of a term. A credential already issued runs out within about 10 minutes.
  • Spell adds no read rules: anyone on a list can read that whole Space with any AT Protocol app, so a rule only Spell's apps obey protects nothing.
  • So which Space a thing is in decides who can see it. What someone mustn't see goes in a Space they aren't on.
  • Spell apps read through a small proxy, because a page likely can't read a Space directly. It checks credentials, forwards, and holds no data, so it's unlikely to go down.

A stricter option: a Space can name which apps may read it, so only Spell's would get a credential. That shuts out other apps and most of the remixing, so it isn't the default.

Writing goes through Spell's write server.

It has to do the writing, not just approve it, because only the holder of the org's right can write to its repo. That's why no other app can get around the check.

Everyone writes to their own repo Spell writes for the org
Enforcing the roles Apps can only ignore a record afterwards Spell refuses it
A Lead deletes someone's post It can only be hidden: it's in their repo It's deleted
Ana edits Bo's row It needs a merge of Spell's own It's the org's row
The last ticket, wanted twice at once Two repos, so nothing can stop both One repo, and the second write is refused
A stranger's program Gets what you granted, unless each has its own identity Asks Spell, which checks that program's rights
The write server is down People can still write Nobody can write, though everyone can still read
Ana leaves the org She keeps what she wrote It stays with the org
Who wrote it, to other apps The protocol says so The org: the person is only Spell's word, in a field

It's still a real Space, with a single writer.

  • The authority's own account may always read and write, under every policy.
  • It stays addressable, and can move hosts.
  • It does set aside the protocol's "authority rests in the author's DID".
  • People can write from their own repos later, as a Space allows many writers.

  • Only what's named is shown: a new field stays private until someone adds it.
  • It can always be rebuilt from the private data, so a failed write is retried, and nothing is lost.

The same data can go outside a Space too:

  • Printing invitations: a page laid out for paper, with nothing new on the server.
  • A phone's wallet: as far as I know, passes must be signed on a server, so this is real server work. It's unchecked.
  • At the door: a printed code can be copied, so an organizer's page checks it against the attendee list.

Each person gets one private Space in the org, their inbox, and everything in it is a pointer to a Spell page.

  • It's made when first needed, has no roles or pages of its own, and only that person is on its list.
  • Each pointer has a title, a line of text, and any private facts the page needs, as a page sees only what its viewer can see.
  • It's worked out when viewed, so it's always current, and its links stay live.
  • Only trusted pages can be sent: a page runs as the person viewing it, so it must be one a Lead put in the org's spaces. Anyone else sends plain messages.
  • A record of what was said needs a fixed version: a message that must never change points to one git version of its page.
  • The number of Spaces grows with the number of people, which is untested.

A plain message is a pointer to Spell's own message page, with its text in the record.

  • It's copied into both people's inboxes, as email is, so deleting yours leaves theirs.
  • A message to several people is one copy each, and a shared id draws a conversation as one thread.
  • It can't be edited once sent, and if the second write fails, Spell retries it.

owen@spell receives messages inside the community, and can forward them.

  • Each person chooses: a phone alert, one email per message, digests, or nothing.
  • An email has the title, the line of text and a link, as it can't run a Spell page.
  • Mail from outside doesn't come in.

Inboxes keep other members out, not the org.

  • They're in the org's repo, so whoever holds its account or runs its host can read them, and people should be told so.
  • That's fine for the first version, and may change.

RS is one org, @rs, on an identity of its own that survives people coming and going.

  • Its standing teams are Administrators, Council, and team leads for membership, events and finances.
  • The events team also approves event proposals.
  • Each quarterly event has its own organizers: event leads and event team leads.
  • It's a trusted group, which is why the first version can rely on that in places.

The master copy is in the event's private space, and the public space gets a projection of it.

  • Organizer notes stay private: "get someone more reliable on this shift as well" is never in the projection.
  • A sheet can be drafted: nothing is public until it's published.
  • The last place on a shift is safe: Spell checks it against the master copy, in one repo.
  • Later rules, like "only people on the attendee list", fit where Spell checks a sign-up.
  • There are no team spaces: every team's pages sit in the event's two spaces, for simplicity, and an event team lead may not be able to change a narrowed page.
  • Notes are still about real people: every Lead of that space, and whoever runs the servers and the host, can read them.

A new event is copied from a template, and the template and these stages are a Spell program RS can change.

Invitations aren't a permission: they're a rule that counts.

  • How many someone may request depends on their standing in the group, plus what they volunteered for.
  • Tickets run out, and two people may ask for the last one at once.
  • A request goes into the event's private space through Spell, and the count reaches the person's inbox as a projection.

The first version trusts the page, and the write server guards only the ticket count.

  • A Spell program runs in the browser, so a page could be made to ask for more than its allowance.
  • Organizers see every request, which is enough for a group that assumes everyone is a good actor.
  • A rule that runs on a server comes later. Running people's code on a server is an epic of its own.

The letter is one page with personal parts, in a third Space for the event's attendees. Being confirmed puts someone on its list.

The letter says Where it comes from Link goes to
The rules and event details The attendees' Space, the same for everyone
"You have signed up for the following shifts", or "You haven't volunteered" The published sheet, which they can already read The sign-up page
"You have used 2 of your available invitations" Their inbox, as this count is private The invitations page

Chat is one more app in a space: a thread is a page, and each message is a record. A similar org runs its events in Discord this way.

Thread Where it sits
Public, and the event's public thread The event's public space
Organizers, and team leads The event's private space
Event leads only A small extra private Space, as a thread can't be hidden from the event's other Leads
  • Volume: one account writes for the whole org, so a busy org's chat could reach the host's write limit.
  • Speed: Spell tells open pages at once, which suits chat, and live rooms are there if that's not enough.
  • Behaviour-crisis notes: they go in the org's own private space, for Admins and Council. Whoever runs the servers and the host can still read them, so RS should know that, or keep them elsewhere for now.
  • The recovery key: whoever leaves holding it holds the group's identity, so more than one person should. How the protocol does that is unchecked.
  • Leaving: a post staying with the group suits a community, but someone who leaves still needs a way to remove their own.
  • Number of Spaces: eight to start, then two or three for each event, and one inbox for each person who gets one.

Spell's declaration stays the only thing anyone writes, and Lexicon, AT Protocol's schema language, is generated from it.

  • Spell already generates JSON Schema from a declaration this way, so Lexicon is one more output.
  • Importing other people's Lexicons as Spell types is what makes remixing work.
Spell Lexicon What to do about it
a recipe is a thing A record, named like spaces.spell.x.recipe A name belongs to whoever controls its domain, and spell.spaces is ours
Text, date, list, one of String, datetime, array, known values Mostly direct
A decimal number None: floats are banned Store it as a string or a scaled integer
A thing inside a thing, saved once, then referenced A nested object, or an at:// link Each record is capped at 1 MiB, and whole-then-reference has no direct equivalent
Rename a field, upgrade on read Not allowed once published: new fields must be optional, with no renames or type changes Field names freeze only on publishing, and a breaking change needs a new name
  • A draft in Spell can change freely, and the spec suggests marking a name experimental until it's published.
  • What a translated (Spanish) program's field names should be, in a record, isn't worked out.

A Spell file or a document is a real file: a blob holds its contents, and a record describes it.

What it is Where it lives Its versions
A file: Spell code, a document, a picture A blob, and one record with its name, type and size, pointing to that blob Git
The file you're editing One draft, saved over and over None: it's replaced each time
The rows an app saves One record per thing, with a schema generated from the app's declarations None: sign-ups and posts just change
An app many orgs use Published in public records, with a record in each org's space pointing to one version Git, then a published version

What happens as you edit:

  • A file has a name, and can be replaced: the hash is only how a record finds its blob, so nobody sees it.
  • The Space holds only the current version: saving a file 1,000 times leaves one file, and going back comes from git history.
  • Only Spell commits, so git never has a conflict to resolve.
  • Code can change freely: a file record's shape never changes, so only an app's saved rows meet the rule that published names freeze.
  • Several files can change together: the blobs go up first, then one batch updates their records.
  • Who can change code: only Leads make pages, which is also what makes a page trusted. Everyone on a Space's list can read it, so a program can hold no secrets.
  • Running it: the source is compiled for the browser, and the result cached. It isn't the thing that's kept.

What this asks for:

  • Uploading a blob into a Space has to work, and how isn't clear yet, so it's the first thing the trial tests. If it can't, a file's text goes in its record for now, with the same name and steps.
  • A draft is readable by everyone on its Space's list, so a draft of a public page sits in the private space, where only Leads can see it.
  • Git holds a second copy of every file, including private ones, so nobody reaches it except through Spell, which checks that Space's list first. It's the one place Spell does guard a read.
  • History remembers: deleting a file leaves it in git, so removing something for good means rewriting history.
  • A blob's size: the reference host allows up to 300 MB by default.

Reused from AT Protocol: DIDs and handles, hosting, OAuth, account moves, sync and moderation.

Spell runs these, and no host at first:

  • spell.spaces: the site.
  • The write server: signs people in, checks every write, writes as each org, and keeps member lists right.
  • The read proxy: checks credentials and forwards, with perhaps a copy for speed.
  • Git history of every file, which only Spell commits to or reads.

What the write server has to get right:

  • The code is small: a few routes that sign in, check and write. The place server already carries data, and a room's referee already answers for who's in it.
  • Holding the right to write: a break-in could write to every org on it, so each org keeps its identity's recovery key, and can leave.
  • Staying signed in: how long that right lasts is up to each host, says the OAuth spec.
  • Being needed: anyone can run the same code, so no org is tied to Spell's own.
  • Limits: one account writes for everyone. Bluesky's hosts allow about 1,666 new records an hour per account, and whether that covers Spaces isn't known.

The feed runs in the browser, which is what makes "your algorithm" literal.

  • It needs a whole space's records, so it's the likeliest reason for the proxy to keep a copy.
  • That should work for communities of hundreds, though it's untested. Millions would need a server.

Programs never hold anyone's grant, because they ask the servers.

  • What each program may do still needs a design.
  • Each gets its own web origin, so one can't read another's page.

Spell buys hosting, and doesn't run it, so someone else does most of this work.

  • The org's own host is a PDS, a Personal Data Server: the reference one asks for 1 GB of memory, 1 CPU, 20 GB of disk, a domain with a wildcard record, and a mail sender, and suggests that for 1 to 20 users.

To ask them:

  • Do they offer Spaces? No provider says so today, and the hosted alpha server isn't for real use, so the trial runs on a throwaway host.
  • Who handles abuse? Accounts under spell.spaces can post publicly, which brings moderation, terms and a privacy policy.

The fallback is running the reference host ourselves, which is easy to set up. Its docs show passwords and no email link.

An alert on your phone is what makes Discord work, so Spell needs a store app, on both iOS and Android.

  • Installing: the app store is far easier than adding a website to your Home Screen, which iOS requires before a web app can send alerts.
  • Reliability: Android web alerts are reported as comparable to native, but on iOS developers report subscriptions being revoked by themselves, and Apple's service can report success for one that has stopped working. This comes from bug reports and forums, as no measured comparison turned up.
  • Sending: the write server makes every write, so it can tell who's waiting on it. Each person chooses per thread or space.

  • Start: one Spell app, holding the same pages, with an org's name and look inside it.
  • After the first release: a master app per org, branded and customized for it, submitted by that org under its own account.
  • Not checked: Google Play's rules.
  1. A start with no setup: joining takes an AT Protocol account, which most people don't have yet.
  2. Writing without Spell: nobody can write while the write server is down, and it holds the keys.
  3. Owning what you wrote in a group: it stays with the org when you leave, and other apps see the org as its author.
  4. Some remixing: other apps read only the spaces an org lets them.
  5. Holding nothing: Spell keeps the git history of every file, private ones included.
  6. Plain files for rows: saved rows are stored in binary archives, so export to plain JSON is something Spell writes.
  7. Confidentiality and recall: hosts and Spell can read everything, and what's been copied can't be recalled.
  8. Offline: a Space can't work offline, so a data folder stays the local copy.
  9. Instant updates from outside: anyone writing from outside Spell gets "something changed, go fetch". Live rooms stay separate either way.
  10. Freedom in the schema: published names freeze, and there are no floats.
  11. Certainty: Spaces is an alpha, so Spell leans into it without depending on it yet, as "Why not commit yet" says.

Spell aligns with Spaces now, and keeps a way out until it's ready.

  • Its own words: breaking changes are coming, data may be lost, no security review, not for production.
  • Its hosted alpha server is invite only, and is deleted when the alpha ends.
  • Upgrades may not work: "Do not expect that you'll even be able to cleanly upgrade between versions".
  • It's a proposal, "not the final specification", hoping to launch later this year, with a beta around the end of November and no promised date.
  • A host that dies probably takes its Space data with it, as nothing mirrors it the way public records are.

The way out: a Spell host stays for local and offline use, shaped like a Space, with the same addresses and roles. Plain export works from the first version.

Unknown until it's run:

  • How a server holds an org's right to write, and for how long, on a host Spell doesn't run.
  • How to upload a blob into a Space, and whether a replaced blob is cleared away.
  • Whether a page can read a Space directly in a browser.
  • How easy signing up for an account really is, for someone who has never heard of AT Protocol.
  • Any independent security audit: none turned up.

The window is open while they're in alpha, and they ask for reports of what's confusing or breaks.

  • Report back with a working app: one server writing for a whole group, a public and a private Space for each team and event, sign-up without a password, and pushing changes to pages.
  • Offer Lexicon generation: Spell becomes an easy way to write schemas.
  • Build what they asked for, later: the recipes case "deserves a blogpost & an example app of its own", in their words. The family cookbook can be that example.
  • Look at the neighbours: Eurosky's mu Studio ("your brand, your audience, your rules") and Roomy, a community platform on AT Protocol, are close to this, as competitors or partners. Neither has been looked at closely.
  1. Data folders' addresses: a full address like owen@spell/recipes, and a server's minimum polling time.
  2. Statusphere in Spell: Bluesky's example app. Public records only, so it needs no Spaces, and it tests the first real atmosphere write, OAuth against a real host, and using a schema someone else published.
  3. Publishing a guide: one public record, through the same write, using Standard.site, which describes a page and links to it.
  4. Lexicon generation: a Spell declaration becomes a published schema.
  5. A trial for one person: a throwaway host, a small write server, and spaces made for one person, using the same steps an org will. It tests uploading a blob first.
  6. Files: a blob and a record per file, one draft while editing, and git for versions.
  7. The group trial: the same, generalized to one Spell group app modelled on RS: temporary roles, public and private spaces, narrowed pages, projections, one event from proposal to quiet, sign-ups, invitations, and a feed you can edit.
  8. Setting up an org: dead easy on an existing host, and simple on the org's own.
  9. Sign-in: with an AT Protocol account, which the atmosphere account's sign-in already does, one check replacing today's local token for requests and room sockets alike.
  10. Keeping data safe: backups, export, delete and limits.
  11. Chat, and the phone app: threads in a space, a store app for iOS and Android with alerts, and the reporting and blocking the stores require.
  12. Inboxes and messages: one private Space per person, with forwarding to email, as messages or digests.
  • A sheet's own "who may add" setting: companies will want it, and it fits the same check. So does limiting a sheet to an event's attendees.
  • More private messages: an inbox could sit on the person's own host, or the app could encrypt messages.

Not part of this plan: personal spaces.

  • Anyone will be able to create their own on spell.spaces, which has nothing to do with an org's spaces, other than perhaps linking between them.
  • family cookbook is saved in the cloud belongs with them, so that line keeps refusing for now.
  • A data folder stays what runs on your own machine, offline.
  1. Who hosts the git history, and is it one repo per org or per Space? The plan keeps it off the org's host, and doesn't name a provider.
  2. Two Leads editing one file: one draft each, with a warning if the file changed, or one shared draft? The plan says one each.
